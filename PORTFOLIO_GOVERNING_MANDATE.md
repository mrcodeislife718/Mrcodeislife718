# Portfolio Governing Mandate

## Primary objective

**MAXIMIZE THE ECONOMIC VALUE OF THE ENTIRE PORTFOLIO — not the revenue of every individual repository.**

This portfolio contains products, platforms, infrastructure, research systems, open-source leverage, strategic IP, licensing opportunities, creative IP, developer ecosystem components, proof/reputation assets, and direct-revenue businesses. Every repository must be assigned the economic role that maximizes portfolio value rather than being forced into a subscription or standalone company.

## Canonical identity correction

**Airborne = `applied-ai-erp-agent`.**

Airborne and `applied-ai-erp-agent` are the same project. Do not count them as separate portfolio projects.

## Economic role classes

A project may occupy one or more of these roles:

1. **Direct revenue** — SaaS, transactions, marketplace fees, services, enterprise contracts, subscriptions, usage.
2. **Licensing / IP revenue** — runtime licenses, OEM, embedded technology, enterprise licenses, commercial licenses, white-label, strategic licensing.
3. **Open-source leverage** — adoption, developer distribution, credibility, ecosystem growth, standard-setting, integration surface, or funnel into paid products.
4. **Platform / ecosystem leverage** — makes other portfolio products stronger, cheaper, faster, more defensible, easier to distribute, or easier to sell.
5. **Strategic option value** — frontier technology whose future strategic value may exceed its immediate monetization value.
6. **Portfolio support** — internal technology that reduces development, compute, deployment, support, acquisition, or operating cost.
7. **Proof / reputation** — technically significant work that strengthens engineering credibility, sales, partnerships, licensing, recruiting, or authority.
8. **Creative / IP asset** — value captured through ownership, licensing, publishing, distribution, merchandise, adaptation, or derivative rights.
9. **Research asset** — preserved when premature commercialization would reduce long-term scientific or strategic value.

A repository being open source does not make it economically irrelevant. A repository generating revenue does not automatically mean it should be a standalone company. A strategically differentiated system should not be exposed cheaply merely to claim monetization.

## Mandatory four-layer audit

Every substantial repository must be evaluated through all four layers before major structural or commercial conclusions are made.

### Layer 1 — Repository truth

Inspect the actual repository before concluding what it is. At minimum inspect, when available and relevant:

- current/default branch;
- all meaningful local/remote branches visible through GitHub;
- commit history and unmerged work;
- README and architecture documents;
- source code;
- tests and evaluation harnesses;
- migrations and schemas;
- infrastructure and deployment configuration;
- CI/CD;
- APIs;
- authentication and authorization;
- billing, pricing, entitlements, and payment integration;
- AI/model/tool behavior;
- integrations and connectors;
- observability and audit controls;
- security controls;
- feature flags;
- TODO/FIXME/placeholders/mocks/disabled code;
- commercial implementation;
- licensing and open-source posture.

Never infer product function from a repository name alone.

### Layer 2 — Optimal economic role

Determine whether the project should primarily be:

- direct paid software;
- transaction business;
- marketplace/network;
- enterprise infrastructure;
- API/usage product;
- open source;
- source available;
- dual licensed;
- licensed IP;
- OEM/embedded technology;
- white-label technology;
- protocol/standard;
- internal platform infrastructure;
- research asset;
- creative IP;
- proof/reputation asset;
- or a deliberate combination.

Do not force every project into SaaS.

### Layer 3 — Missing opportunity / missing defense

Always investigate beyond the explicit request. Determine:

- What did the owner not ask?
- What has not been implemented that materially matters?
- What future problem can be structurally eliminated now?
- What economic opportunity is being overlooked?
- What dependency could eventually control the product or margin?
- What competitor advantage remains unneutralized?
- What customer objection is not yet designed around?
- What revenue surface has not been considered?
- What open-source choice could increase distribution without exposing the economic core?
- What should stay proprietary?
- What could be licensed instead of operated directly?
- Which portfolio projects should reinforce one another without being merged?
- What maintenance burden should be eliminated?
- What legal, regulatory, tax, security, privacy, IP, support, or operational problem appears if the project succeeds?
- What changes at 10 customers, 1,000 customers, and 1,000,000 customers?
- What happens if adoption is much faster than expected?
- What prevents a larger incumbent from copying, bundling, subsidizing, or commoditizing the product?
- What part of the value chain should the portfolio own?
- What part should intentionally be delegated to a stronger external provider?
- Is there a standards, certification, marketplace, data, transaction, interoperability, or infrastructure position more valuable than simply selling software seats?

### Layer 4 — Execution

When a repository-grounded gap is material and the owner has already authorized implementation, do not stop at describing the gap.

**Fix it.**

The default execution sequence is:

1. preserve existing work;
2. create or use the appropriate branch when structural isolation is warranted;
3. implement the justified change;
4. integrate it with existing architecture;
5. test the affected behavior;
6. resolve resulting defects;
7. document only what is necessary to operate, sell, maintain, or verify it;
8. avoid unnecessary rebuilding of already-completed systems.

Additional engineering, testing, benchmarking, infrastructure work, or experimentation is justified when it directly enables sales, customer delivery, retention, expansion, margin, procurement, security/regulatory acceptance, scale, defensible claims, or preservation of important functionality.

## Mandatory output of every repository audit

Every audit must answer:

**WHAT EXISTS → WHAT IS MISSING → WHAT YOU DIDN'T ASK → WHAT COULD BREAK → WHAT COULD MAKE MONEY → WHAT SHOULD BE OPEN → WHAT SHOULD STAY PROPRIETARY → WHAT SHOULD BE LICENSED → WHAT REINFORCES OTHER PROJECTS → WHAT SHOULD BE FIXED NOW → WHAT SHOULD BE LEFT ALONE → WHAT HAS THE HIGHEST ECONOMIC LEVERAGE**

## Portfolio economic-value equation

Use this as a decision framework rather than fake precision:

**Portfolio Economic Value = Direct Revenue + Recurring Revenue + Licensing + Transaction Economics + Distribution Leverage + Ecosystem Power + Cost Savings + Strategic IP + Standards Influence + Data/Network Effects + Reputation/Proof + Future Option Value**

minus:

**CAC + Operating Cost + Support Burden + Maintenance + Regulatory Exposure + Dependency Risk + Commoditization + Opportunity Cost + Unnecessary Complexity**

## Capital-allocation rule

Allocate attention according to probability-adjusted economic return, while preserving asymmetric upside.

Maintain multiple horizons simultaneously:

- **Revenue now** — shortest legitimate path to paying customers.
- **Recurring growth** — products capable of compounding MRR/ARR, transactions, or marketplace volume.
- **Enterprise/high-ticket** — products capable of large contracts, licensing, infrastructure, or private deployments.
- **Asymmetric strategic upside** — technologies whose long-term IP/platform value may greatly exceed near-term revenue.
- **Open-source distribution** — projects deliberately used to create adoption, ecosystem reach, standards influence, and funnels into commercial value.

Do not let long-horizon research or infrastructure starve near-term cash generation. Do not destroy major strategic assets for trivial short-term revenue.

## Portfolio synergy rule

Analyze every project both independently and as part of the portfolio.

Look for legitimate cross-project reinforcement in distribution, standards, identity, payments, trust, data, evaluation, developer workflow, compute efficiency, operations, media, learning, marketplaces, and enterprise relationships.

Do not merge independent products merely because integration is possible. Preserve separate product boundaries when independent economics are stronger.

## Open-source rule

For each candidate open-source project, explicitly identify:

- what is safe and useful to open;
- what adoption/distribution benefit open source creates;
- what proprietary core must remain protected;
- what paid control plane, hosted service, enterprise layer, certification, marketplace, optimizer, support, or licensing layer captures economic value;
- whether permissive, copyleft, source-available, open-core, or dual-license economics are most appropriate;
- whether open sourcing would destroy a licensing advantage or strategic moat.

Do not open-source protected value by default merely for visibility.

## Licensing rule

For technology with strong embedded, infrastructure, or enterprise value, assess licensing before forcing direct operation. Consider:

- commercial runtime licenses;
- enterprise licenses;
- OEM licenses;
- embedded licenses;
- per-device/per-core/per-seat/per-workload economics;
- private deployment;
- white-label;
- strategic partnerships;
- support/SLA contracts;
- source-available commercial rights;
- field-of-use licensing where appropriate.

## Success-too-well rule

Every commercially serious project must be examined at roughly 1x, 10x, and 100x adoption. Anticipate:

- capacity and cost scaling;
- support burden;
- abuse and fraud;
- security blast radius;
- regulatory thresholds;
- vendor concentration;
- customer concentration;
- reliability and recovery;
- pricing failure;
- operational bottlenecks;
- data/identity/payment risk;
- governance and authority;
- lock-in exposure;
- incumbent retaliation;
- copy/bundle/subsidize attacks;
- capital requirements;
- organizational complexity.

## Governing decision question

When choosing what to work on next, ask:

**Which available action has the highest probability-adjusted impact on revenue, customer acquisition, retention, expansion, margin, distribution, strategic leverage, standards power, cost reduction, licensing value, or enterprise value — while preserving important long-term IP?**

Then execute that action rather than defaulting to novelty, infrastructure-building, repeated architecture work, or testing for its own sake.

## Completion doctrine

The portfolio lifecycle is:

**BUILT + TESTED → ECONOMIC ROLE CHOSEN → COMMERCIALIZED / OPEN-SOURCED / LICENSED / INTEGRATED AS APPROPRIATE → DISTRIBUTED → USED → VALUE CAPTURED → RETAINED / ADOPTED → EXPANDED → SCALED → DEFENDED**

The goal is not to maximize the number of monetized repositories.

The goal is to maximize the durable economic power of the portfolio.
